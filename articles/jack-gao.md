# Five Minutes Ahead

Last June, junior Jack Gao was attending a science camp in Oklahoma when five tornadoes ripped through the area in a single week. The funnels didn't shake him so much as annoy him. The warning system, he discovered, was almost useless.

"How we issue tornado warnings is god-awful," Gao said. "Every day the National Weather Service will be like, 'Here's a general risky area,' and this area will be the size of a state. From there, it's just people on the ground spotting the tornado."

The gap Gao describes has bothered meteorologists for decades. A tornado watch arrives five or six hours before severe weather, but a warning — the actual confirmation that a funnel is on the ground — averages only ten to fifteen minutes of lead time. That figure isn't generous. It's roughly how long a tornado lasts.

"We're like, it's five miles away, it takes ten minutes to get to us, and that's your lead time," Gao said. "We don't actually predict anything."

Gao has worked at George Mason University's Center for Ocean, Land, and Atmosphere Studies since freshman year, first on ocean heat flux off the coast of South America and then on a spatial-temperature analysis whose figures landed in Virginia's official climate assessment. After Oklahoma, he turned to tornadoes. The standard forecasting models are generative — they evolve the atmosphere forward in time, step by step — and at the scale of a tornado, roughly 200 meters across, even someone clapping their hands seeds errors that compound into a butterfly effect within minutes. Gao threw out the prediction-by-simulation playbook. The model he built pairs a convolutional neural network with a random forest classifier, bridged by a principal-component step that lets spatial features survive into the decision tree. He says the combination is the first of its kind for weather data. It trains on Doppler radar tagged by researchers at MIT's Lincoln Laboratory, with a polar-coordinate range mask bolted on because radar doesn't think in Cartesian grids. Where the National Weather Service's own 2019 CNN underperformed existing methods, Gao's posts a 68 percent improvement on CSI, the gold-standard metric in tornado forecasting. Run continuously against the 2023 Memphis outbreak, the third-deadliest in U.S. history, it caught 13 of 14 tornadoes with a maximum lead time of 20 minutes and an average of seven.

"Pre-tornadic structures are fundamentally identifiable and differentiating from tornadoes themselves," Gao said. "That's big."

The implications run past accuracy numbers. Working with his professors and a coach at the National Weather Service, Gao believes his ablation analysis offers the first observational evidence of the Rotunno-Klemp-Wiseman hypothesis, a forty-year-old theory describing how horizontal vorticity tilts vertical to form a tornado. The structure had been simulated but never seen in radar data. Roughly nine in ten of the EF-4s in his test set crossed an 80 percent probability threshold, which suggests the model has a feel for severity, not just presence. Gao is now leading the publication. The model that started on his laptop runs on a supercomputer, and he has already been asked to apply the same approach to dust storms.

"Tornadoes are sort of like the million-dollar problem of weather forecasting," Gao said. "If you're able to crack tornado forecasting, you can apply that model to so many other things, because it's solving the inherent paradox of high resolution and low error rate. Those are fundamentally opposing forces. But if you're able to develop a model that's able to do this, then that's just going to be the best model for everything."
